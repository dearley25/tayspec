Security Controls & Cryptography

Part 3 of a Security+ (SY0-701) study series. This part covers the two ways Security+ categorizes controls, then moves into cryptography — encryption, hashing, digital signatures, and the certificate infrastructure (PKI) that makes trust on the internet possible.

What are security controls?

A security control is anything used to reduce risk by protecting systems, data, or people. Controls don't eliminate threats or guarantee safety — what they do is reduce the likelihood of an attack, reduce its impact, detect attacks early, and help recovery after an incident. Security+ groups controls two different ways, and both groupings matter.

By purpose

  • Preventive — stops an attack before it happens: firewalls blocking traffic, encryption, strong authentication, ACLs, least privilege. Reduces likelihood.
  • Detective — identifies attacks that are happening or already happened: logs, SIEM systems, IDS, alerts. Reduces time to detection.
  • Corrective — fixes problems after detection: restoring from backups, patching, re-imaging infected machines, resetting credentials. Reduces impact and recovery time.
  • Deterrent — discourages an attacker from trying at all: cameras, guards, warning banners, lighting, signage. Works by increasing accountability and relying on fear of detection or punishment.
  • Compensating — used when the primary control can't be implemented, because of a legacy system, budget constraint, or compatibility issue. Example: a legacy system that can't be encrypted gets compensated for with network isolation, restricted access, and increased monitoring; a system that can't support MFA gets compensated for with strong password policy and login-attempt limits.
Exam clue words

"Cannot implement," "legacy system," "not supported," and "alternative control" are all signals the question is testing compensating controls specifically.

By type

  • Technical — enforced by technology: firewalls, antivirus/EDR, encryption, IDS/IPS, MFA. Automated, consistent, and scalable — but can't fix poor human decisions.
  • Administrative — policies, rules, and procedures: security policies, acceptable use policies, training programs, background checks, incident response plans. Shapes behavior and is often required for compliance, but relies on people actually following it.
  • Physical — protects facilities and hardware directly: locks, badges, fences, cameras, access control vestibules. Prevents physical access, but doesn't stop a purely cyber attack.
  • Operational — how security is executed day to day: people following processes, supported by technology. Log review and monitoring, incident response procedures, and doing backups all fall here.

Cryptography basics

Three terms worth pinning down before anything else: plaintext is normal, human-readable data; a cipher is the algorithm and key used to transform it; ciphertext is what you get after plaintext has gone through the cipher — unreadable without the key.

Encryption converts plaintext into ciphertext using an algorithm and a key. Only someone with the correct key can decrypt it back — which means encryption protects data even if an attacker gets direct access to it.

Symmetric encryption

The same key encrypts and decrypts. It's very fast and efficient for large data sets, but it has a real weakness: the key distribution problem — how do you securely share that one key in the first place? Examples: AES (128/192/256-bit) and the older DES. Common uses: disk encryption, database encryption, and the data-transfer phase of a VPN tunnel.

Asymmetric encryption

Uses a key pair — a public key that's shared openly and a private key that's kept secret. Slower than symmetric encryption, but it solves the key-exchange problem and enables identity verification. Examples: RSA and ECC. Common uses: HTTPS handshakes, email encryption, digital signatures.

In practice the two are combined: asymmetric encryption securely exchanges a key, then symmetric encryption handles the actual data transfer. This combination is called hybrid encryption.

Hashing

Hashing converts data into a fixed-length value and is strictly one-way — it cannot be reversed. It's used to verify integrity and to store passwords securely. Examples: SHA-256, MD5 (now considered insecure). Hashing is not encryption: there's no key, and nothing to decrypt.

Digital signatures

Digital signatures provide authentication, integrity, and non-repudiation. The sender hashes the message, then encrypts that hash with their private key. The receiver decrypts it with the sender's public key and compares hashes — a match proves the message is authentic and wasn't altered in transit.

Digital certificates and PKI

If encryption protects data, PKI protects trust. Encryption alone never answers "how do I know who I'm talking to?" — without PKI, anyone could claim to be a bank or send a "secure" message with no way to verify it. PKI exists to solve identity verification and trust at scale.

A digital certificate is a trusted digital ID that binds a public key to a verified identity — "this public key really belongs to this person, server, or organization." It contains a public key, the subject (owner name), the issuer (the Certificate Authority), a validity period, a serial number, and the CA's digital signature. Private keys are never inside certificates.

What happens during an HTTPS handshake

  1. The server sends its certificate.
  2. Your browser verifies the certificate.
  3. The browser trusts the public key.
  4. The encrypted session begins.

If trust fails at any step, the browser shows a warning instead.

Key fields of a certificate

  • Subject — who the certificate belongs to (Common Name / domain, Organization, Country). If the subject doesn't match the site's domain, the browser warns.
  • Issuer — the trusted Certificate Authority that issued it. Browsers only trust a certificate if they trust its issuer.
  • Serial number — a unique ID from the issuing CA, used to track and revoke the certificate.
  • Validity period — "not before" and "not after" dates. An expired cert means an untrusted connection.
  • Public key — used to encrypt data and verify signatures. The private key is never included.
  • Signature algorithm — the algorithm the CA used to sign the certificate (e.g. RSA with SHA-256, ECDSA with SHA-256). A weak algorithm is itself a security risk.
  • Digital signature — created by the CA with its own private key, proving the certificate hasn't been altered. Browsers verify it with the CA's public key.
  • Extensions — define what the key is allowed to do: Key Usage, Extended Key Usage (e.g. server or client authentication), Subject Alternative Name (modern browsers rely on SAN rather than CN for the domain match), and CRL Distribution Points (where to check revocation).

PKI vocabulary

  • Public Key Infrastructure (PKI) — the framework of hardware, software, policies, and procedures used to create, manage, distribute, validate, and revoke digital certificates.
  • Public key — shared openly; encrypts data and verifies signatures.
  • Private key — kept secret; decrypts data and creates signatures. If a private key is compromised, trust in everything it signed is lost.
  • Certificate Authority (CA) — a trusted organization that verifies identities, issues certificates, and signs them.
  • Registration Authority (RA) — verifies the requester's identity before the CA issues a certificate. The RA validates; the CA issues.
  • Chain of trust — the hierarchical relationship Root CA → Intermediate CA → end-entity certificate. If any link breaks, trust breaks.
  • Root certificate — a self-signed certificate that serves as the ultimate trust anchor.
  • Intermediate certificate — issued by the root CA and used to issue end-entity certificates, so the root CA itself is never directly exposed.
  • CRL (Certificate Revocation List) — a list of revoked certificates, published by a CA and checked periodically.
  • OCSP (Online Certificate Status Protocol) — checks a certificate's real-time validity instead of relying on a static list.

Data states

Data exists in three states, and each needs its own protection strategy.

  • Data at rest — stored data (hard drives, databases, backups). Protected with disk encryption, database encryption, access controls, and physical security — the goal is protecting data even if the storage itself is stolen.
  • Data in transit — data moving across a network (HTTPS, VPN, email). Protected with HTTPS, VPNs, and secure email protocols — the goal is preventing eavesdropping and tampering.
  • Data in use — data actively being processed (RAM, open files). Protected with memory protection, secure enclaves, access controls, and endpoint security — the goal is protecting data while it's actually being accessed.

Practice question

Question

Q: During an SSL handshake, which entity validates the certificate signing request (CSR) on behalf of the certificate authority?
A: Registration Authority (RA) — the RA is certified by a root CA and authorized to validate CSRs for specific uses. PKI itself is a hierarchy of computers that issue and manage certificates using asymmetric key pairs. During the handshake, the client checks the certificate's validity period, confirms the issuing CA is on its trusted list, and uses the CA's public key to verify the CA's signature on the server's certificate. To guard against man-in-the-middle attacks, the client also compares the server's actual DNS name to the DNS name on the certificate.

Glossary

Security controls

  • Security control — any safeguard used to reduce risk by protecting systems, data, or people. Reduces likelihood, impact, or improves detection/recovery — never eliminates threats outright.
  • Risk — the potential for loss or damage when a threat exploits a vulnerability.
  • Threat — anything capable of causing harm to a system, network, or organization.
  • Vulnerability — a weakness that could be exploited by a threat.

Cryptography

  • Cryptography — securing data using mathematical techniques to ensure confidentiality, integrity, and authenticity.
  • Plaintext — readable, unencrypted data.
  • Cipher — the algorithm and key used to transform plaintext into ciphertext.
  • Ciphertext — unreadable data produced after encryption.
  • Encryption / Decryption — converting plaintext to ciphertext, and back again, using an algorithm and a key.
  • Symmetric encryption — one key for both encryption and decryption (AES, DES).
  • Key distribution problem — the challenge of securely sharing a symmetric key without interception.
  • Asymmetric encryption — a public/private key pair (RSA, ECC).
  • Hybrid encryption — asymmetric encryption for key exchange, symmetric encryption for the actual data transfer.
  • Hashing — a one-way process that converts data into a fixed-length hash value (digest); SHA-256 is current, MD5 is no longer considered secure.
  • Digital signature — provides authentication, integrity, and non-repudiation using asymmetric encryption.
  • Non-repudiation — assurance that a sender cannot deny having sent a message.

Data states

  • Data at rest — stored data.
  • Data in transit — data moving across a network.
  • Data in use — data actively being processed in memory or an application.

Quick reference

  • Controls are layered and overlapping — by purpose and by type
  • Cryptography supports CIA plus trust
  • Encryption is reversible with a key; hashing is not reversible at all
  • PKI answers "who am I really talking to?"
  • Data must be protected in all three states: at rest, in transit, in use
