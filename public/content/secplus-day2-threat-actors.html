Threat Actors & Attack Types

Part 2 of a Security+ (SY0-701) study series. Security begins with understanding the attacker. This part covers who attacks systems, why attacks happen, how attackers approach targets, and the broad categories of attack every specific technique falls under.

What is a threat actor?

A threat actor is any person, group, or entity that can cause harm to systems, networks, or data. They can be external or internal, and accidental or intentional — threat actors aren't always hackers; sometimes they're employees or ordinary users who make a mistake.

Understanding threat actors helps an organization predict attack methods, prioritize defenses, assess risk accurately, and design the right controls. Different attackers require different defenses — security is contextual, and a bank and a school face very different threats.

Threat actor classification

Threat actors are typically classified by skill level, motivation, resources, and access level. These categories help predict behavior, not morality.

  • Script kiddies — low-skill attackers who rely on pre-built tools and scripts. Limited technical knowledge, opportunistic, and they target easy or unpatched systems. Despite the low skill ceiling, they can still cause serious damage.
  • Hacktivists — politically or socially motivated attackers focused on promoting a cause, disrupting services, or exposing information. Common tactics: website defacement, DDoS, data leaks. Hacktivists chase visibility, not profit.
  • Organized crime — highly structured, financially motivated groups that steal data, commit fraud, and deploy ransomware. Skilled attackers running long-term campaigns with professional tooling — most ransomware attacks trace back to organized crime.
  • Nation-state actors — government-sponsored attackers motivated by espionage, intellectual property theft, or infrastructure disruption. Extremely skilled, well-funded, and persistent (this is where the term APT — Advanced Persistent Threat — comes from). Nation-state attacks can go undetected for long periods.
  • Insider threats — employees, contractors, or vendors, split into malicious, negligent, and compromised insiders. Insiders already have legitimate access, which makes them especially dangerous.

Threat actor motivation

Motivation drives behavior: financial gain, political influence, espionage, revenge, or curiosity and challenge. Motivation helps predict attack type — understanding why helps predict how.

Threat vectors and attack surface

A threat vector is the path an attacker uses to gain access — email, web applications, removable media, wireless networks, supply chain. It describes how the attack enters, not the payload it delivers.

The attack surface is every possible entry point into a system: open ports, user accounts, APIs, applications, physical access points. Smaller attack surface means lower risk, and security hardening works by reducing that surface.

Major attack categories

Most specific attacks fall into one of five categories, and real-world attacks often combine more than one.

  • Social engineering — exploits human trust, lack of awareness, and emotional response. Examples: phishing, pretexting, impersonation, tailgating. Humans are often easier to exploit than systems.
  • Malware-based attacks — malicious software delivered via email attachments, downloads, infected websites, or USB drives. Types include viruses, trojans, ransomware, and spyware. Malware usually requires execution, not just delivery.
  • Network-based attacks — target data in transit, network availability, or network trust relationships. Examples: DDoS, man-in-the-middle, ARP poisoning, DNS spoofing. These often exploit weak configurations.
  • Application attacks — exploit poor coding and input validation flaws. Examples: SQL injection, cross-site scripting (XSS), command injection, buffer overflow. Many breaches start with a vulnerable web app.
  • Physical attacks — target hardware, facilities, and devices directly. Examples: theft, tailgating, hardware tampering, dumpster diving. Physical security is part of cybersecurity, not separate from it.
Gotcha

Real-world attacks often chain multiple categories together and occur in stages — a phishing email leads to malware, which leads to lateral movement across the network. Attackers think in steps, not single actions, so defense has to address the whole chain, not just one link of it.

Practice questions

Each question below mirrors the scenario-based style used on the actual exam — read the scenario, pick the principle it's testing, then check the explanation.

  • Q: A company encrypts all customer data on its servers and restricts access using permissions. Which CIA triad principle is most directly protected?
    A: Confidentiality — encryption and access controls prevent unauthorized access, which directly protects confidentiality.
  • Q: An employee logs in successfully but can't modify system settings due to their role. Which concept is being enforced?
    A: Authorization — authorization determines what actions a user is allowed to perform after logging in.
  • Q: An attacker uses automated tools downloaded from the internet to scan random IPs for open ports and known vulnerabilities. Which threat actor is most likely responsible?
    A: Script kiddie — script kiddies rely on pre-built tools and target easy, random systems.
  • Q: A hospital's ransomware attack encrypts patient records and blocks staff from accessing them. Which CIA principle is most affected?
    A: Availability — the data still exists but can't be accessed, meaning availability has been disrupted.
  • Q: Which situation best represents a risk: a hacker group existing on the internet, an unpatched server, a firewall blocking traffic, or an unpatched server exposed to the internet?
    A: An unpatched server exposed to the internet — risk requires both a threat and a vulnerability to be present at once.
  • Q: An attacker emails an employee pretending to be IT support and convinces them to reset their password. Which attack category is this?
    A: Social engineering — the attacker exploits human trust, not a technical flaw.
  • Q: A company assigns users only the permissions necessary to do their jobs. Which principle is being followed?
    A: Least privilege — limiting access to only what's required reduces potential damage.
  • Q: Which threat actor is most likely to run long-term, stealthy attacks focused on espionage rather than financial gain?
    A: Nation-state actor — well-funded, highly skilled, and typically focused on espionage.
  • Q: Which control best represents accounting in the AAA model?
    A: Activity logs and audit trails — accounting tracks what actions users performed after gaining access.
  • Q: A company installs firewalls, IDS, and endpoint protection together. Which security strategy is this an example of?
    A: Defense in depth — multiple overlapping security layers working together.
  • Q: Which attack-surface reduction strategy would most effectively lower overall risk: more log retention, removing unused applications and services, adding more users, or disabling encryption?
    A: Removing unused applications and services — fewer entry points means a smaller attack surface and lower risk.
  • Q: A politically motivated group launches a DDoS attack against a government website to make a public statement. Which threat actor type is most likely responsible?
    A: Hacktivist — driven by political or social causes, not profit.
  • Q: An employee accidentally clicks a malicious link, causing malware to execute. Which insider threat type does this represent?
    A: Negligent insider — the action was unintentional, caused by a lack of awareness.
  • Q: Why is cybersecurity described as risk management rather than risk elimination?
    A: Threats will always exist — security reduces likelihood and impact, but risk can never reach zero.
