Security Fundamentals

Part 1 of a Security+ (SY0-701) study series. Before any of the attacks, tools, or controls in the rest of this series make sense, you need the vocabulary and mental model that everything else gets built on: why security exists, what it's actually trying to protect, and the handful of core concepts — the CIA triad, AAA, least privilege, defense in depth, zero trust, and risk — that every later topic traces back to.

What is cybersecurity?

Cybersecurity is the practice of protecting computers, networks, applications, data, and users from unauthorized access, data theft or leakage, data modification, and service disruption. It focuses on risk management, not perfect protection — cybersecurity is defensive, and most real-world jobs are about protection, detection, and response rather than stopping every possible attack.

Why cybersecurity exists

Systems store valuable information, stay constantly connected, and attract attackers actively looking for weaknesses — and mistakes happen. The common consequences of poor security are financial loss, legal issues, loss of trust, and business downtime.

Key idea

Most attacks succeed because of misconfigurations or human error, not advanced hacking.

Assets we must protect

An asset is anything with value that needs protection: user accounts and credentials, files and databases, servers and endpoints, network devices, cloud resources, and applications and services.

The CIA triad

The CIA triad is the three core goals of cybersecurity. Every security control supports at least one of these — and if one part of the triad fails, security has failed.

  • Confidentiality — only authorized users can access data. Enforced through encryption, authentication, access control lists, and permissions. Failures here mean data breaches, privacy violations, and identity theft. Confidentiality doesn't guarantee accuracy or uptime — only secrecy.
  • Integrity — data is accurate and hasn't been altered improperly. Enforced through hashing, digital signatures, and file integrity monitoring. Integrity protects against unauthorized modification, not viewing.
  • Availability — systems and data are accessible when needed. Enforced through backups, redundancy, failover systems, and DDoS protection. An unavailable system is effectively useless, even if it's perfectly secure.

Example — an online email system: confidentiality means only the owner can read their emails, integrity means emails aren't altered in transit or storage, and availability means the email service stays up. Security decisions often involve balancing the three — increasing one can reduce another, like encryption adding overhead that costs performance.

The AAA security model

AAA describes how users interact with systems — networks, servers, applications, and cloud environments all rely on it. It controls who gets access, what they can do, and what they did.

  • Authentication — answers "who are you?" Methods fall into something you know (password, PIN), something you have (smart card, phone), or something you are (biometrics). Multi-factor authentication (MFA) combines two or more factors. Authentication proves identity only — it does not grant permissions.
  • Authorization — answers "what are you allowed to do?" Enforced through file permissions, user roles, and admin privileges. Authorization happens after authentication — logging in successfully doesn't mean full access.
  • Accounting — records logins, file access, changes made, and system activity, used for auditing, troubleshooting, incident response, and compliance. Without logs, investigations are nearly impossible.

Principle of least privilege

Users receive only the access required for their job, limited in both scope and time. This reduces the impact of a compromise, limits insider threats, and shrinks the attack surface. Administrative access should be rare and tightly controlled.

Defense in depth

Defense in depth stacks multiple, overlapping layers of protection — a firewall plus an IDS, MFA plus strong passwords, network segmentation plus access controls. The logic: an attacker only needs to find one weakness, but a defender needs enough layers that no single failure is fatal.

Zero trust

Zero trust assumes no system is trusted by default and verification is always required, both inside and outside the network. Its core principles are strong authentication, least-privilege access, and continuous monitoring.

Understanding risk

Risk is the possibility of loss from a threat exploiting a vulnerability. Security work is about identifying risk, reducing risk, and accepting the risk that's left over — risk can never be reduced to zero.

  • Threat — anything capable of causing harm: hackers, malware, natural disasters, insider actions. Threats exist whether or not defenses are in place.
  • Vulnerability — a weakness in a system or process: unpatched software, weak passwords, misconfigured systems, risky user behavior. A vulnerability raises risk even if no attack ever exploits it.
Gotcha

Risk only exists where a threat and a vulnerability meet. A threat with no matching vulnerability is not a risk, and a vulnerability with no threat against it is not a risk either. Security controls exist to reduce risk — removing the vulnerability, reducing the threat, or limiting the impact — not to eliminate threats outright.

Quick reference

  • CIA triad: Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability — every control supports at least one
  • AAA: Authentication (who), Authorization (what), Accounting (logged)
  • Least privilege: minimum access, minimum time
  • Defense in depth: multiple overlapping layers, no single point of failure
  • Zero trust: never trust by default, always verify
  • Risk = threat × vulnerability; risk is managed, never eliminated
