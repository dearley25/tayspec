Incident Response & Forensics

Part 5 of a Security+ (SY0-701) study series. Incident response is the process for handling a security incident — minimizing damage, stopping the attack, restoring systems safely, and learning from what happened. It's not purely technical: it involves people, process, and technology together. This part walks through the six-phase IR lifecycle, then digital forensics — the discipline of proving, not just stopping, what happened.

The six phases of incident response

1. Preparation

Everything that happens before an incident: an Incident Response Plan (IRP), defined roles (incident handlers, management, legal, communications), contact lists and escalation paths, security tooling (SIEM, EDR, IDS/IPS, centralized logging), tested backups, and training through tabletop exercises. The mindset here is that you assume an incident will happen — being prepared is what keeps that moment from turning into chaos.

2. Identification

The question being asked is "is this actually an incident?" — detection plus validation. Alerts get reviewed, logs get analyzed, Indicators of Compromise (IOCs) get checked, and scope and impact get estimated. Examples: a malware alert from antivirus, unusual outbound traffic flagged by a SIEM, multiple failed logins, or a user reporting a suspicious email. Not every alert is an incident — false positives are common, and this phase exists to sort out which is which.

3. Containment

Damage control — stopping the spread, not fixing everything yet.

  • Short-term containment — isolate the infected system, disconnect it from the network, disable the compromised account.
  • Long-term containment — temporary fixes, firewall rules, network segmentation, configuration changes.
Exam trap

Containment is not eradication. Stopping the bleeding and removing the threat are two different phases, and the exam will test whether you know which is which.

4. Eradication

Now the threat actually gets removed — the attacker should no longer exist anywhere in the environment. This includes removing malware, deleting malicious files, closing the exploited vulnerability, patching systems, and re-imaging compromised machines when necessary. If eradication is incomplete, the attacker comes back.

5. Recovery

Systems return to normal operation — carefully. Restore from known-good backups, verify system integrity, monitor for reinfection, and gradually reconnect systems to the network rather than all at once. Monitoring doesn't stop once systems are back online; recovering too fast is what creates reinfection risk.

6. Lessons learned

Happens once everything is stable again — and it's the phase most often skipped in real life. It produces a post-incident report, a timeline of events, what worked and what failed, and updates to policies, controls, training, and the IRP itself. Every incident should leave the organization's security posture better than it found it.

Exam clue words by phase
  • Preparation — "before an incident," "planning," "policies," "training"
  • Identification — "detected," "alert triggered," "unusual activity observed"
  • Eradication — "remove," "eliminate," "clean systems"
  • Recovery — "restore," "return to production," "monitor for recurrence"
  • Lessons learned — "documentation," "review," "improvements"

Chain of custody

Documents exactly who handled a piece of evidence, ensuring it stays untampered and admissible in court. This matters whenever legal action is possible or law enforcement may get involved — a broken chain of custody can make otherwise solid evidence worthless.

Digital forensics

Digital forensics is the process of identifying, preserving, analyzing, documenting, and presenting digital evidence — focused on what happened, how it happened, and who was involved. It often overlaps with incident response, but the two have different jobs: incident response stops the attack; forensics understands and proves what happened.

Its goals: preserve evidence integrity, reconstruct events, identify the attacker's actions, support legal or disciplinary action, and prevent future incidents.

The forensics process

Security+ doesn't mandate one exact model, but the typical flow commonly aligns with NIST guidance. Order matters, because evidence has to remain trustworthy at every step.

  1. Identification — determine what systems are involved and what data might be evidence (hard drives, RAM, logs, network traffic, mobile devices). You're locating potential evidence here, not analyzing it yet.
  2. Preservation — prevent evidence alteration. Don't modify original data, isolate systems if needed, maintain timestamps, use write blockers. Examples: removing a hard drive and storing it securely, or disconnecting a system from the network without powering it off. Evidence integrity beats convenience, every time.
  3. Collection — create forensic copies rather than working on originals, using bit-by-bit imaging, and hash the data before and after collection so any change is detectable. The original evidence stays untouched; all analysis happens on a copy.
  4. Examination — extract relevant data, filter out noise, recover deleted files, and review logs, metadata, and artifacts. Still mostly technical work — conclusions haven't formed yet.
  5. Analysis — interpret the evidence, build a timeline, and determine what happened, how it happened, and the scope of compromise. This is where conclusions start forming.
  6. Reporting — document the findings clearly, accurately, and reproducibly, written for management, legal teams, and possibly a court. Poor documentation makes otherwise good evidence useless.

Order of volatility

Data disappears at different speeds, so volatile data has to be collected first:

  1. CPU registers / cache
  2. RAM
  3. Network connections
  4. Running processes
  5. Disk storage
  6. Backups / archives

Forensic tools

  • Autopsy — open-source, GUI-based digital forensics platform. Windows, macOS, Linux.
  • FTK Imager — creates forensic images with read-only access. Lightweight and simple, Windows.
  • Volatility — command-line memory forensics tool that analyzes RAM dumps, extracting running processes, network connections, malware in memory, and injected code.
  • Wireshark — packet capture and analysis, used across networking and forensics. Reveals captured traffic, credentials sent in cleartext, DNS queries, and suspicious outbound connections.
  • Plaso — builds forensic timelines by pulling from logs, browser history, and file metadata, correlating events. Windows, Linux, macOS.
  • Cellebrite UFED — industry-standard mobile forensics, used by law enforcement and enterprises. Extracts SMS, app data, and location history from iOS, Android, SIM cards, memory cards, and IoT devices.
  • HashCalc — a simple hashing utility for MD5/SHA integrity checks. Windows.

Quick reference

  • IR phases: Preparation → Identification → Containment → Eradication → Recovery → Lessons Learned
  • Containment stops the spread; eradication removes the threat — they are not the same phase
  • Forensics: Identification → Preservation → Collection → Examination → Analysis → Reporting
  • Volatile data first: CPU/cache, then RAM, then network, then processes, then disk, then backups
  • Analyze copies, never originals — hash before and after to prove integrity
  • Incident response stops the attack; forensics proves what happened
